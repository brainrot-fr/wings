NIST: what I read and what it changes

The 63A and 63C PDFs in context are the 2017 versions, and both carry NIST’s withdrawal notice (withdrawn Aug 1, 2025, superseded by the -4 editions). I’m treating them as history only, and I’m not opening the old 63b or 63-3 for the same reason. The one that matters for wings is 63B-4, and it confirms last turn’s password claims:

Length: a password used as a single factor needs at least 15 characters. One used only inside MFA needs at least 8. wshobson’s 12 matches neither.
Maximum length: verifiers should allow at least 64 characters.
Composition and rotation: composition rules are banned, and so is periodic rotation. A forced change is allowed only on evidence of compromise.
Blocklist: passwords are checked against a blocklist of breached, common and context-specific words, such as the service name or username. The whole password is compared, and the reason for rejection must be shown.
Storage inputs: no truncation, NFC normalization before hashing, no stored hints, and no security questions or KBA.
Usability and throttling: password managers and paste are allowed, and rate limiting is mandatory (§3.2.2).

My earlier “12-character minimum” criticism was the wrong target. The composition rules are the real violation, and 12 also falls short of NIST’s 15.

One inference I haven’t verified: no-truncation conflicts with bcrypt’s 72-byte cap, so the auth rule should default to Argon2id. I’ll check that against OWASP Password_Storage before writing it down.
